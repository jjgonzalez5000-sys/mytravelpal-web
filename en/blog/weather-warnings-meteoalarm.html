Weather warnings on a road trip: how to read MeteoAlarm and what to do on the road

Checked on 2026-10-26

A forecast tells you whether it will rain. A warning tells you whether the weather could hurt you. On a long drive across Europe that difference matters: the sunny morning in Lyon can turn into an orange wind warning on the Rhône valley by the afternoon, and a family car with a roof box or a motorhome feels it long before a saloon does. This guide explains where official European warnings come from, what each colour really means, and what national met services and road authorities advise drivers to do when the colour goes up.

What MeteoAlarm is

MeteoAlarm is the European portal for official weather warnings. It is part of EUMETNET, the network of European national meteorological services, and is coordinated by GeoSphere Austria. It currently gathers the warnings of 44 national meteorological and hydrological services, among them AEMET in Spain, Météo-France, the Deutscher Wetterdienst (DWD) in Germany, MeteoSwiss, GeoSphere Austria, IPMA in Portugal and the Italian Air Force met service.

The key point for travellers: MeteoAlarm does not write its own warnings and does not change the ones it receives. Each country issues its warnings, and MeteoAlarm puts them on one map with a shared colour code. That makes it the quickest way to see a whole cross-border route at once, while the national service remains the place for the full detail. The map shows the next three hours by default, and you can switch to the next 24 hours or to any of the next five days.

The colours and what they officially mean

MeteoAlarm uses three warning colours. The descriptions below paraphrase its website guide:

ColourMeaningWhat MeteoAlarm asks of you
Yellow (moderate)Potentially dangerous weather. The phenomena are not unusual.Pay attention if your plans expose you to the weather, stay informed and avoid risks you can avoid.
Orange (severe)Dangerous weather. Unusual phenomena are expected; damage and casualties are likely.Be very vigilant, check the detailed forecast regularly and follow the advice of the authorities.
Red (extreme)Very dangerous weather. Exceptionally intense phenomena, major damage and accidents likely, often with a threat to life, over a wide area.Keep frequently informed, follow the orders and advice of the authorities in all circumstances and be ready for extraordinary measures.

Where there is no warning, the area is shown without colour (transparent), meaning no particular awareness is needed. Some national services do use green for that state on their own maps. Dark grey marks countries whose service does not take part in MeteoAlarm.

Warning types that matter for drivers

MeteoAlarm groups warnings into hazard types. Its guide covers wind, snow or ice, thunderstorm, fog, high temperature, low temperature, coastal event, forest fire, avalanche, rain, flood, rain flood, marine hazard and drought. For a road trip, these are the ones to look at first:

  • Wind: gusts are singled out as a risk to transport. This is the main warning for vans, motorhomes, caravans and anything with a roof box.
  • Snow or ice: snow cover, ice on wet surfaces and freezing rain, with slippery roads and poor visibility.
  • Thunderstorm: lightning, heavy rain, strong gusts and hail.
  • Fog: the guide names road safety directly as an impact.
  • Rain and rain flood: intense or prolonged rain, including the risk of aquaplaning, and fast-rising water over a small area in a short time.
  • High temperature: long hot spells, made worse when the nights stay warm.
  • Forest fire: weather that favours fires (heat, low humidity, wind), which matters on wooded mountain roads in summer.

How the national services fit in

Spain, AEMET (Meteoalerta). AEMET issues warnings at three levels: yellow when the danger is low («be alert»), orange when it is significant («be prepared») and red when it is extraordinary (act on the instructions of the authorities). Its plan covers rain, snow, wind, storms, maximum and minimum temperatures, coastal phenomena, dust, fog, snowmelt and avalanches, plus local phenomena such as galernas and rissagues.

France, Météo-France (Vigilance). The vigilance map has four colours: green (no particular vigilance), yellow (be attentive), orange (be very vigilant) and red (absolute vigilance). It is updated at least twice a day, at 06:00 and 16:00, and shows the highest level for today and tomorrow. It covers wind, waves and submersion, rain and flooding, river floods, storms, snow and ice, avalanches, heatwave and extreme cold.

Germany, DWD. The DWD uses four levels: yellow (official warning), orange (warning of marked weather), red (severe weather warning) and violet (warning of extreme severe weather, with possible danger to life). It also publishes separate heat and UV warnings and a hatched «advance information» when severe weather is possible but its place and timing are not yet certain. Because MeteoAlarm shows a single three-colour scale, it is worth opening the DWD page itself if you are driving in Germany during a severe spell.

What official advice says to do at orange and red

Météo-France publishes behaviour advice for each phenomenon. A selection for drivers:

  • Wind: at orange, limit your journeys; at red, do not use your car.
  • Snow and ice: at orange, fit your vehicle with special equipment, carry food and blankets and limit journeys; at red, if you get stuck, leave the vehicle only when the authorities tell you to.
  • Thunderstorms: at orange, stay informed and avoid travelling; at red, do not use your vehicle, and if you are already driving, go at walking pace and never enter a flooded road.
  • Rain, flooding and river floods: at orange, avoid travelling and never drive onto a submerged road, even partly submerged; at red, do not use your car.

Spain’s traffic authority, the DGT, adds practical driving advice:

  • Strong wind: hold the wheel with both hands, watch flags and trees for gusts, expect a push after passing a truck or leaving a tunnel and slow down. Boxy, high-sided vehicles are more affected, and on very windy days the DGT says it is better not to set out towing a trailer or caravan; if you must, slow down and check the mirrors for sway. A tailwind also makes it easy to exceed the speed limit without noticing.
  • Snow and ice: adapt your speed to the conditions rather than the limit, increase the distance, be gentle with steering, throttle and brakes, and watch shaded stretches. Chains must match the tyre size and go on the driving wheels. Carry a scraper, a torch, a blanket, gloves, food and drink.
  • Fog: switch on the fog lights, do not use high beams, use the rear fog light only in dense fog and keep a much longer gap.
  • Heavy rain and flash floods: drive slower, increase the distance considerably, and if a road is flooded, turn around and look for another way. If you are trapped by water, call 112.
  • Extreme heat: avoid driving between 13:00 and 17:00, stop every two hours or 200 km, drink plenty of water and never leave people or animals in the car.
  • Wildfires: never try to drive through if the fire has crossed the road.

The DGT’s general rule fits every country: check official channels for warnings and road conditions before leaving, and if the route looks bad, look for an alternative, postpone or cancel.

Winter equipment: Austria, as an example

Snow warnings go hand in hand with equipment rules, and those differ by country. In Austria, the motor vehicle act (KFG, section 102, paragraph 8a) says that from 1 November to 15 April, when roads are wintry (snow, slush or ice), cars may only be driven with winter tyres on all wheels. Snow chains on at least two driving wheels are an accepted alternative only where the road is covered by a continuous or almost continuous layer of snow or ice. Check the rules of every country on your route before a winter trip; they are not the same everywhere.

A simple routine before and during the trip

  • The day before: open MeteoAlarm for every country you cross, then the national service for any area showing orange or red.
  • On the morning: check again. In France the vigilance map is refreshed at 06:00, so a quick look before breakfast is enough.
  • Think in hours, not days: a warning may cover the afternoon only. Leaving earlier, or stopping for lunch before the front, can be enough.
  • Keep a plan B day: on a multi-day trip, a flexible overnight stop lets you wait out a red warning instead of driving into it.
  • Carry reserves: a full tank, water, food, warm clothes and a charged phone, so a closed road becomes an inconvenience rather than an emergency.
  • Motorhomes and high-sided vehicles: treat an orange wind warning as a reason to reconsider exposed viaducts, mountain passes and coastal roads.
