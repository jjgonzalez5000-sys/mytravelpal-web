Breaks on a long drive: the two-hour rule explained

Checked on 2026-10-26

Most road trips go wrong in a quiet way. Nobody plans to drive tired: you set off late, the motorway is empty, the kids are finally asleep and stopping feels like losing time. Two hours become three, then four. Road safety authorities across Europe have a simple answer for this, and it is worth knowing exactly what they say, where the advice comes from, and how it differs from the legal limits that truck and coach drivers must follow.

What the official recommendations say

Spain’s traffic authority, the DGT, is direct in its advice on driving with sleepiness or tiredness: always stop every 2 hours or every 200 km, and make those breaks last at least 20 to 30 minutes. Its trip planning guide words it slightly more loosely, as a rest every two hours or roughly every 200 to 300 km, used to stretch, relax and walk around. The DGT also says to stop more often when conditions are unfavourable.

The UK Highway Code (rule 91) recommends a minimum break of at least 15 minutes after every two hours of driving, tells you not to start a journey tired, to get enough sleep before a long one and to avoid long journeys between midnight and 6 am, when natural alertness is at its lowest.

The figures differ a little, but the message is the same: two hours is a ceiling, not a target. Neither rule is a legal obligation for private drivers; they are safety recommendations, and the authorities stress that fatigue can arrive well before the two-hour mark.

Why it matters: fatigue and microsleep

The DGT links sleepiness to between 15 and 30% of traffic accidents in Spain, and its page on fatigue gives a similar range. Two points in its material deserve attention.

First, you do not choose when you fall asleep. The body eventually forces sleep, and the DGT says plainly that the moment cannot be controlled. Second, microsleeps last only a few seconds and usually go completely unnoticed by the driver. A few seconds at motorway speed is a long stretch of road covered with nobody in control.

The DGT also names the times of day with the highest risk: between 3 and 5 in the morning and between 2 and 4 in the afternoon. The afternoon window is the one families tend to forget, right after a big lunch.

Warning signs you should not ignore

The DGT lists a long set of symptoms. The ones most drivers recognise:

  • Eyes: blinking more often and for longer, eyelids half closed, blurred vision.
  • Body: heaviness, pressure in the temples, neck and back pain, tingling or cramps in arms and legs.
  • Movements: constant changes of posture, stretching, yawning, drumming on the wheel, singing or whistling to stay awake; fewer and less precise steering corrections.
  • Behaviour: driving on autopilot, irritability, taking more risks than usual.
  • Reactions: slower reaction time and misjudging what other drivers are doing. The DGT adds that many sleepy drivers drift towards the centre of the road or to the left.

If you notice any of these, the recommendation is the same everywhere: stop in a safe place. The Highway Code specifically says not to stop on the hard shoulder of a motorway or in an emergency area; use a rest area, a service area or the next exit.

Power naps and coffee

Once sleepiness has appeared, the DGT says the safest option is to stop and sleep in a suitable place for about 20 to 30 minutes, and advises against naps longer than that, because a long nap lowers performance and can disturb your night’s sleep. A short walk and fresh air during a break help you stretch, but they do not replace sleep when your eyes are closing.

On stimulants, the DGT is cautious: it advises against coffee and other stimulants after 18:00, because their effects can last until bedtime, and asks you to use them carefully to avoid a rebound effect. It also notes that leaning more and more on coffee, tea or energy drinks to get through the day is a sign of chronic fatigue that deserves a doctor’s opinion. In short: coffee is not a plan, sleep is.

The professional benchmark: EU Regulation 561/2006

Truck and coach drivers work under binding rules. As summarised by the European Commission, under Regulation (EC) No 561/2006:

  • a break of at least 45 minutes must be taken after 4.5 hours of driving at the latest, and it can be split into 15 minutes followed by 30 minutes;
  • daily driving time may not exceed 9 hours, extendable to 10 hours no more than twice a week;
  • weekly driving may not exceed 56 hours, and 90 hours over two consecutive weeks;
  • daily rest is at least 11 hours, reducible to 9 hours up to three times a week.

These rules do not apply to someone driving their own car on holiday. The regulation covers the carriage of goods by vehicles above 3.5 tonnes and the carriage of passengers in vehicles built for more than nine people including the driver, and it explicitly exempts vehicles up to 7.5 tonnes used for the non-commercial carriage of goods. It has been amended since 2006, so check the current consolidated text if you drive for work.

Notice how the numbers compare. The professional rules allow 4.5 hours before a break, more than double the two hours recommended to the public. They are a legal maximum for trained drivers on a regulated schedule, not a comfortable pace for a family. Still, they are a useful benchmark: if a professional may not drive more than 9 hours a day on most days, planning a 12-hour stage on a single holiday day deserves a second thought.

What about motorhomes and vans?

A motorhome used privately for a holiday is neither a goods transport business nor a passenger service, and the scope described above is built around those activities. If your vehicle is used commercially, for instance in a rental or delivery business, ask the national authority which rules apply. Either way, the recommendations for private drivers still make sense, and motorhome drivers have extra reasons to follow them: a tall, heavy vehicle is more tiring in crosswinds, on mountain roads and in narrow towns. Plan shorter stages, factor in a slower average speed, and keep a rest day in the plan for long trips.

Practical planning: where and how to stop

  • Plan the stops before you leave. Stopping is much easier when the next rest area is already chosen. On motorways, service areas give you fuel, toilets and food; simpler rest areas are quieter for a short sleep.
  • Eat light. The DGT recommends light, easily digested meals and warns that heavy meals cause drowsiness. Never pair them with alcohol. Put the main meal stop at a natural break point rather than carrying on straight after eating.
  • Share the driving. If two people can drive, swap at breaks rather than waiting until the driver feels tired. The co-pilot who is awake also helps by watching for the warning signs.
  • Children. Children set their own rhythm: plan stops with space to run around, and use breaks to check that every child is properly secured in a seat suited to their size.
  • Pets. Animals need water, shade and a short walk as well. Use the same stops for them and never leave a pet in a parked car in the heat.
  • Watch the clock, not just the km. Avoid starting the longest stretch in the early hours or straight after lunch, and plan to arrive before you are exhausted, not when you finally are.
